D1 · Educational problem

Students who fail “Floating and sinking” usually hold intuitive rules — “heavy things sink, light things float” and “big things sink, small things float” — that rote re-reading of the textbook does not change. For a repeat learner these beliefs come with anxiety: every red cross confirms “I am not a science person”, so she avoids practice. She needs a safe place to be wrong: a lab where her own prediction is tested, where a failed experiment is treated as evidence rather than a mark against her, and where she discovers for herself that it is mass compared with volume (density), not mass or size alone, that decides floating. Because she only gets her mother’s phone for a few minutes, each visit must be one complete 10-minute mission that resumes exactly where she stopped.

Learning outcomes

  1. LO1 – Predict whether an object will float or sink in water by comparing its density with water’s density (1 g/cm³).
  2. LO2 – Calculate an object’s density from its mass (balance) and volume (displacement), in g/cm³.
  3. LO3 – Explain, using evidence from her own experiments, why neither mass alone nor size alone decides floating (e.g. a 3 kg log floats but a 16 g iron nail sinks).

Context of use

At home, about 9:00–9:15 pm after dinner, on her mother’s shared Android phone (360 px wide, patchy 4G), alone, one 10-minute mission per evening; also usable on the school computer-lab desktop.

D3 · Educational content (one complete learning activity)

Concept explanation (shown after she computes density herself, built from her own data)

“Let us look at your own evidence: (1) The wooden log (3.0 kg) floated. So it is not weight alone. (2) The iron nail (only 2 cm³) sank. So it is not size alone. (3) What decides is how much mass is packed into each cm³. Scientists call this density = mass ÷ volume. 💧 Water has 1 g in each cm³. Less than 1 → floats. More than 1 → sinks. Cutting iron smaller does not change its density — that is why the tiny nail still sinks.”

Lab data (physics computed in code, not by AI)

Objects (g/cm³): wood 0.5, apple 0.8, candle wax 0.9, potato 1.08, rubber eraser 1.3, pebble 2.6, iron 7.9 · Sizes: tiny 2, small 20, big 500, huge 6000 cm³ (mass = density × volume) · Liquids: tap water 1.0, very salty water 1.2, cooking oil 0.92. A floating object sits with a fraction ρobject/ρliquid under the surface (e.g. wood “50% under”).

  • One complete sequence of 7 states budgeted at 10 min (0.5 + 1 + 1 + 3.5 + 1.5 + 2 + 0.5). Header shows “Step 3 of 6 · Lab · about 8 min left” — an estimate, deliberately not a countdown (L5 anxiety).

D7 · Pedagogical rationale

How the design supports learning

  • Predict–Observe–Explain + conceptual change. Every drop requires a prediction first. A discrepant result (the 3 kg log floating) creates the dissatisfaction with M1/M2 that conceptual-change theory says is needed; density is introduced only afterwards, when it is intelligible (she computes it from her own numbers) and fruitful (it explains every row in her notebook).
  • Discovery from her own data. The concept card is generated from her experiments (“The wooden log (3.0 kg) floated…”), and the density table is sorted with a water line at 1 g/cm³, so the rule is seen, not told.
  • Topic-specific tools (balance, measuring cylinder/overflow can, liquid picker, notebook) and a physically correct float depth (wood 50% under, wax 90% under) let her reason with real quantities.
  • Control of variables is taught implicitly through mission presets (same material, different sizes) and hint rung 3.

Feedback and scaffolding

  • Fading hint ladder (4 rungs): notice → compare → change one variable → measure both. The rung rises with each failed prediction or “I’m stuck” (performance + interaction adaptivity).
  • Misconception-specific feedback on every check item, always pointing back to her own lab evidence; model answer shown after each item.
  • Adaptive rule in Check: misconception answer → targeted feedback + a twin item; misconception again on the twin → “Show me in the lab” with the decisive pair preloaded, then back to the check.
  • Profile adaptivity: warm-up answers + hypothesis choose the lab mission (mass-mission vs size-mission).

Answer to the P5 design challenge

How will the system support learning from an unsuccessful experiment without simply revealing the correct answer?

  1. Reframe: a failed prediction is shown in amber as “🔍 A surprise! Scientists love these. This is not a mistake — it is new evidence.” Never red, never “wrong”.
  2. Question, don’t tell: the support is a single Socratic question tied to her objects and numbers (AI-generated, or the offline ladder). Answer-revealing words (density, mass ÷ volume, g/cm³) are blocked by a filter for rungs 1–3; rung 4 only tells her what to measure, never the rule.
  3. Agency: she chooses “✏️ Change my idea” (new hypothesis version, v1 → v2 shown and celebrated) or “🧪 Test more first”.
  4. The answer emerges from her data: the word “density” appears only after she computes mass ÷ volume for one of her own objects in the Discover step.

Shown in D5(b): the wooden log prediction fails → surprise card with hint 1 of 4 → second failure (nail) raises it to hint 2 → she revises her idea.

How the learner context (L5) shaped the design

  • No scores, timers, red crosses or “wrong”; warm-up is explicitly “no marks”; outcomes shown as “✔ Strong / ↻ Practise once more”.
  • Confidence (self-efficacy) is asked before and after and the change is reflected back: “That came from your own evidence” (mastery experience).
  • Twin items give a second chance instead of a penalty; worked solution on the second calculation attempt.
  • Short sentences, conversational “Let us…”, highlighted key phrases, numbered cues; Marathi/Hindi words accepted in free text.

How the constraint (C4) shaped the design

  • Content was cut to one concept (density vs water) and one transfer teaser; salt water is a next-session mission (spacing).
  • Seven short states with a time budget; a calm “about N min left” estimate instead of a countdown.
  • Save & stop everywhere + exact resume, because her phone time is borrowed and gets interrupted.
  • No login and no install: opening the link is the whole onboarding, so the 10 minutes are spent learning.

Practical fit

About 50 KB of code, no images or video, so it loads on patchy 4G; works without AI (labelled offline hints); data stays on the device; one-handed big buttons at 360 px.

Reflection. I used AI as a fast builder and a critic, but kept the decisions that carry the learning design. I chose the card for fit, not novelty: a deterministic simulation guarantees correct physics, while AI is limited to language tasks where it is strong and where errors are recoverable. I did not trust AI output blindly: every AI feature has a validator (enum checks, answer-leak filter, rubric cross-check) and a labelled fallback, and the D5 paths work even with AI switched off. The persona, misconceptions, feedback wording and the “no red crosses” rule came from thinking about Sneha’s anxiety and borrowed phone time; I reviewed and edited AI-written content for tone, simple language and scientific accuracy (density values checked against textbook figures). A limitation is that the AI hint quality depends on the model and was only spot-checked; a teacher moderation sample is part of the evaluation plan.
